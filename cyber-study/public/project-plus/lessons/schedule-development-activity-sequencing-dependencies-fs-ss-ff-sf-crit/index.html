Explain it simpler

A project schedule is a list of tasks in the right order, with dates. Some tasks must wait for others to finish, like painting a wall only after the plaster dries. If you line up all the chains of tasks, the longest chain decides how soon the whole project can finish. That chain is the critical path. Any delay on it delays everything. Tasks off the critical path have some spare time, called float. To finish sooner, you can add more people or pay for faster delivery on critical tasks, which costs money and is called crashing. Or you can do tasks at the same time that you planned to do one after another, which is called fast-tracking and raises the risk of redoing work. Speeding up a task that is not on the longest chain does not help.

The sponsor of the new Willow Creek Clinic branch network has just told you the opening date moved up: you have 15 days, not 18. Your engineers immediately offer to speed up the site survey, because it is the first thing on the list and they can finish it in half the time with an extra person. It sounds helpful. But when you look at the network diagram, you suspect that finishing the survey early will not move the opening day by even an hour. Why not, and where should the extra money and effort actually go?

Start with how a schedule is built. A schedule turns the work breakdown structure (WBS) into dated activities. The project manager lists the activities needed to produce each work package, identifies milestones, estimates durations, then sequences them and finally assigns dates and resources. Sequencing is drawn as a network diagram, usually with the precedence diagramming method (PDM), where boxes (nodes) are activities and arrows are dependencies. The finished schedule is often shown as a Gantt chart, a bar chart of activities against a calendar, which is easier for stakeholders to read than the network diagram.
