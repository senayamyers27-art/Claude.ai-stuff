Explain it simpler

Agile is a way of running projects in small pieces. Instead of planning everything at the start, the team builds a little, shows it to users, learns and adjusts. Scrum is the most popular agile method. It works in short, fixed blocks of time called sprints, usually a few weeks long. The product owner decides what to build first, the scrum master coaches the team and clears obstacles, and the developers decide how to build it. Work is written as short user stories, like 'As a shopper, I want to save my cart so I can finish later'. Velocity is how much work a team usually finishes per sprint, used to plan ahead. Kanban is a different agile method with no sprints: work flows across a board, and you limit how many items are in progress, like a kitchen that only cooks a few orders at a time.

You have just joined Lantern Health's self-service portal team as a project coordinator. On day one, the security lead walks up to a developer mid-sprint and asks for a new login screen by Friday. The developer looks at the scrum master, who looks at you. Across the room, the IT operations team is drowning in unpredictable tickets and wonders whether two-week sprints even make sense for them. And a director has asked why your team's velocity is 28 when another team's is 45. Is the security lead's request allowed, is the director's comparison fair, and which way of working fits the operations team?

Start with the agile mindset. Agile approaches deliver value in small, frequent increments and adapt as they learn, instead of fixing all requirements up front. They suit work where requirements are expected to change, such as new software or a customer-facing portal, and where users can give regular feedback. The Agile Manifesto values individuals and interactions over processes and tools, working software over comprehensive documentation, customer collaboration over contract negotiation, and responding to change over following a plan. The items on the right still matter; the items on the left matter more. That is why agile does not mean no plans or no documentation.
