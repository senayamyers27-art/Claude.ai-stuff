Planning: estimating time and cost (analogous, parametric, three-point/PERT, bottom-up) and setting baselines

Explain it simpler

Estimating is making an educated guess about how long work will take and what it will cost. There are a few ways to do it. You can compare with a similar job you did before. You can multiply: if one task takes an hour, forty take forty hours. You can estimate every small piece and add them up, which is slow but most accurate. Or you can think of the best case, the most likely case and the worst case, and blend them. Once everyone agrees on the numbers, they become the baseline, the official version you measure progress against. It is like planning a road trip: you estimate drive time, agree on an arrival time, then check along the way whether you are ahead or behind.

The steering committee at Granite Valley Schools wants one number: how long will it take to roll out the new device management agent to every staff laptop? Your lead technician says 'about six weeks, probably.' Another engineer, who has been burned before, says 'could be three months if the integration fights us.' The superintendent is already writing 'six weeks' in her newsletter draft. You know that once a single date is printed, it will become a promise. You also know you have numbers from last year's rollout and a pilot starting next week. How do you turn guesses into an estimate you can defend, and then lock it in as something to measure against?

Once work packages and activities are known, the team estimates how long each will take and what it will cost. Estimates are best made by the people who will do the work, because they know the details and are more committed to numbers they helped create. Every estimate should state its assumptions and a range, because every estimate is uncertain. Accuracy usually improves as the project progresses, which is why early estimates are rough (a rough order of magnitude, or ROM) and later ones are refined into budget and definitive estimates. This refining as information arrives is called progressive elaboration. Present an estimate as a range, such as 8 to 10 weeks, rather than a single date whenever you can, so stakeholders understand the uncertainty instead of treating a guess as a promise.
