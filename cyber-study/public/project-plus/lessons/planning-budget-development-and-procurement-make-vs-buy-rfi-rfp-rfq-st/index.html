Explain it simpler

A project budget is the total of every cost estimate, plus a safety cushion for risks you can see coming. Some costs are big one-time purchases, like buying servers, and some are ongoing bills, like a monthly cloud subscription; companies track these differently. When the team needs outside help, it first decides whether to build or buy. If buying, it can ask vendors three kinds of questions: 'What is out there?' (information), 'How would you solve our problem and for how much?' (proposal), or 'What is your price for exactly this item?' (quote). The chosen vendor's work is described in a statement of work. Contracts decide who pays if costs run over, a bit like a fixed-price car repair versus paying the mechanic by the hour.

The invoice on your screen from the contractor helping Elmwood Library migrate its catalog is three times what you budgeted. You call the vendor's account manager. She is polite and unbothered: the contract is time and materials, there is no cap, and your team kept asking for 'just one more thing' by email. Meanwhile, finance has asked why the new cloud subscription was filed as a capital purchase, and procurement wants to know why a request for quote went out for a system nobody had designed yet. Three problems, all rooted in decisions made months ago in planning. Which choices would have prevented each one?

The project budget is built by adding up the cost estimates for activities and work packages, then adding the contingency reserve for identified risks. That total, spread over time, is the cost baseline. It is often drawn as an S-curve, because spending starts slowly during planning, speeds up during execution when most people and purchases are active, and tails off at the end. The management reserve for unknown risks is added on top to form the total project budget, but it is held by management and released only with their approval. Budgets include labor, hardware, software licenses, cloud subscriptions, facilities, training, travel and vendor costs.
