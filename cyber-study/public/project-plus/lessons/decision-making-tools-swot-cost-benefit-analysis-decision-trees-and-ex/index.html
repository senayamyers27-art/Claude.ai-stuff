CompTIA Project+ PK0-005 · Domain 3: Tools and documentation

Decision-making tools: SWOT, cost-benefit analysis, decision trees and expected monetary value, brainstorming and root cause analysis

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed October 7, 2026 · Leer en español

Explain it simpler

Projects are full of choices: which option to buy, which supplier to use, and why something keeps breaking. Decision tools are simple, structured ways to think those choices through so they are fair and easy to explain. A SWOT grid sorts good and bad points into things inside your control and things outside it. Cost-benefit analysis adds up what something costs and what it gives back. Expected monetary value means multiplying how likely something is by how much money it would cost or save. Brainstorming gathers lots of ideas before judging any of them. Root cause analysis keeps asking why until you find the real reason a problem happens. It is like deciding whether to buy a used car: you list its good and bad points, compare the price with the savings, and ask the mechanic why the engine light keeps coming on.

It is Monday afternoon at Pinewood Logistics, and the sponsor of your warehouse database migration wants an answer by Friday: do the migration with your own staff, or pay a specialist vendor. Priya from infrastructure insists the team can handle it. Marcus from finance points out that the vendor quote is fifteen thousand dollars higher. Meanwhile the shipping app has crashed three times this month, and each time someone simply restarted the server. Everyone has an opinion, and the loudest voice in the room is winning. You need a way to turn opinions into a choice you can explain and defend later. Which tools do you reach for, and how do you show the sponsor the numbers instead of the noise?

Projects involve a constant stream of decisions: which option to fund, which vendor to pick, how to respond to a risk, why a problem keeps coming back. Structured decision tools make those choices more objective, easier to explain to stakeholders and easier to defend later, for example when an auditor or a new sponsor asks why the team chose one path over another. The exam tests whether you can match each tool to its purpose and, for expected monetary value, do the arithmetic. As you read, notice that each tool answers a different question, and that question is the clue the exam gives you.
