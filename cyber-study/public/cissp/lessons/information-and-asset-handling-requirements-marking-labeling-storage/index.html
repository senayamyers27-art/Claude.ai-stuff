Information and asset handling requirements (marking, labeling, storage)

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed October 7, 2026 · Leer en español

Explain it simpler

Imagine a pharmacy where every medicine bottle has a colored cap: green means anyone can buy it, red means a pharmacist must hand it over. The cap only helps because everyone knows what each color means and what to do. Handling requirements are the same idea for information. A classification level, such as Confidential, is the color. A label is the sticker on the container, like a backup tape or laptop. A marking is the notice inside the content, like a line at the bottom of each page. The rules then say how each level must be stored, sent, used and thrown away. If you find something with no sticker at all, you play it safe and treat it as the most sensitive thing it could be.

You are covering the front desk at Harbor Credit Union when a facilities worker hands you a plain black USB drive he found under a conference table. No sticker, no name, no asset tag. Down the hall, the overnight backup tapes are waiting for the courier, each with a label that reads only an inventory number. A loan officer has just asked whether she can email a member's file to her personal account so she can finish it at home. Three small moments, one question underneath all of them: how is anyone supposed to know what this information is worth and what they are allowed to do with it?

Classification only helps if people and systems can tell what level a piece of information has and know what to do with it. Handling requirements translate each classification level into concrete rules for marking, labeling, storing, transmitting, sharing and destroying information and the media that carries it. Without them, a classification scheme is just a list of words on a policy page.
