ISC2 CISSP 2024 outline · Domain 5: Identity & access management

Identification, authentication and authorization; MFA and passwordless

Explain it simpler

Getting into a system happens in steps. First you say who you are, like typing your username. That is identification. Then you prove it, like entering a password or tapping a security key. That is authentication. Then the system decides what you may do, like letting you read files but not delete them. That is authorization. Finally, the system keeps a record of what you did, so actions can be traced to you. That is accountability. Proof can come from something you know, something you have or something you are. Using two different kinds, like a bank card plus a PIN, is multifactor authentication. Passwordless sign-in replaces the password with a key stored on your device that a fake website cannot trick into working.

It is just after midnight when Marcus, a senior engineer at Fernhill Software, is woken by his phone buzzing. A sign-in approval prompt. He taps deny. Another arrives, then another, a dozen in five minutes. Half asleep and assuming a glitch, he taps approve to make it stop. By morning, someone has used his account to download source code. His password was strong and the company had MFA turned on. The incident review starts with an uncomfortable question from the security director: if we already had multifactor authentication, what exactly did we get wrong?

Access control follows a sequence that the exam expects you to know cold. Identification is the subject claiming an identity, such as typing a username or presenting a badge. Authentication is proving that claim. Authorization is deciding what the authenticated subject may do. Accountability, which depends on auditing, ties actions back to the individual. Together these are often summarized as IAAA. If identities are shared, accountability collapses, because you can no longer tell who did what, which is why unique user IDs matter as much as strong passwords.
