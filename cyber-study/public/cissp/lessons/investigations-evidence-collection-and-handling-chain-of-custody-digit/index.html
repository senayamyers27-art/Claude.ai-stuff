Investigations: evidence collection and handling, chain of custody, digital forensics tools and techniques

Explain it simpler

An investigation is a careful search for what really happened, and evidence is the proof. Digital proof is fragile. Just opening a file can change the date it was last viewed, and turning a computer off erases everything held in its short-term memory. So investigators grab the most fragile information first, make an exact copy of the hard drive without changing the original, and do all their digging on the copy. They also keep a signed log of every person who handled each item. Think of a sealed bag of evidence on a TV police show: everyone who touches it signs a sheet, so nobody can later claim someone swapped what was inside.

It is 4:40 p.m. on a Friday at Cedar Valley Logistics when the data loss prevention console lights up: a payroll analyst's workstation is pushing large compressed archives to a file-sharing site nobody recognizes. Her manager is already walking toward the desk, saying he will just open the laptop and see what is in those files. HR wants a meeting on Monday. Legal has not heard about any of it yet. You are the responder on call, and whatever you touch in the next ten minutes may end up described in a courtroom months from now. What do you do first, and what must you make sure nobody does?

Security operations teams are often the first to discover an incident that turns into an investigation. The matter might end as an internal disciplinary action, a civil lawsuit, a regulatory inquiry or a criminal prosecution, and you rarely know which at the start. That uncertainty is the reason for the discipline in this lesson: evidence must be collected and handled from the first minute in a way that preserves its integrity and admissibility, so every legal option stays open. Mistakes made in the first hours, such as a manager browsing files on a suspect laptop, can change timestamps and make evidence useless later, no matter how damning it looked.
