ISC2 CISSP 2024 outline · Domain 3: Security architecture & engineering

Information system lifecycle: stakeholder needs through retirement

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed October 7, 2026 · Leer en español

Explain it simpler

Every computer system has a life, from the first idea to the day it is switched off for good. First, you ask the people who will use and run it what they need, including how to keep it safe. Then you turn those needs into clear, testable rules, design it, build it, check it, launch it, run it for years and finally retire it safely. Security should join at the very start, because fixing a problem on paper is cheap and fixing it after launch is expensive. Two checks are easy to mix up: verification asks whether you built it the way the plan said, and validation asks whether it actually helps the people it was built for. Building a house works the same way: plans, inspections and, one day, a careful demolition.

Two weeks before launch, the project manager for Harborview County's new benefits portal emails Leila in security: could she 'quickly sign off' on the system? Leila opens the documents and finds no security requirements, no threat model and a test plan that checks whether pages load but not who can see whose records. The developers are proud of the code, the users have never seen it, and the county administrator expects to announce it next month. Leila can stop the launch, wave it through, or try to fix things in a hurry. How did the project reach this point, and where should security have entered the story?

Security is cheapest and most effective when it is built in from the start and carried through to the end of a system's life. The CISSP outline describes an information system lifecycle drawn from systems engineering practice, such as ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288 and the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Special Publication (SP) 800-160. It runs from understanding stakeholder needs through retirement. The same logic applies whether you build software in-house, buy a commercial product or configure a cloud service. Think of the lifecycle as a loop rather than a straight line, with security activities attached to every stage: requirements and risk assessment at the start, threat modeling in design, secure coding and testing during build, authorization at launch, monitoring and change control in operations, and sanitization at the end.
