Explain it simpler

After a system knows who you are, it has to decide what you are allowed to do. There are several ways to make that decision. In one, the owner of a file decides who can see it, like lending your notebook to a friend. In another, the system uses strict labels like Secret and Top Secret, and nobody, not even the owner, can break the rules. In another, your job title decides your access, so all nurses get nurse permissions. In another, one rule applies to everyone, like a store that is closed to all customers after 9 p.m. Some systems look at many details at once, such as who you are, what the file is, where you are and what time it is. Others calculate how risky a login looks right now.

At Marigold Regional Hospital, three complaints land on your desk in the same morning. A billing clerk could open clinical notes she has no reason to see. A researcher shared a folder of patient data with a visiting student by right-clicking and adding her name. And a cardiologist travelling abroad was blocked at 3 a.m. and is furious. The chief medical officer wants a single answer: who decides who sees what in this hospital? You realize the honest answer is several different systems, each deciding in a different way. Which model is behind each complaint, and is each one doing its job?

Once a subject is authenticated, an authorization mechanism decides what it may do. The CISSP exam tests several models, and questions usually turn on two things: who makes the access decision, and what the decision is based on. If you learn each model by those two questions, most scenario questions become straightforward. Real organizations mix models, so expect scenarios where different layers of the same system use different ones.
