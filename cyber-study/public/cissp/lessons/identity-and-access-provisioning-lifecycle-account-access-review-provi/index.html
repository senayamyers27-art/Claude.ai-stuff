ISC2 CISSP 2024 outline · Domain 5: Identity & access management

Identity and access provisioning lifecycle: account access review, provisioning and deprovisioning

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed October 7, 2026 · Leer en español

Explain it simpler

Every person at work gets a set of digital keys: logins to email, file shares, apps. Those keys should change as the person's life at work changes. When someone starts, they get only the keys their job needs. When they change jobs, they hand back the old keys and get new ones. When they leave, all their keys are taken away right away. Every few months, a manager looks at the list of who holds which keys and confirms each one still makes sense. Think of a gym membership: you get a card when you join, the card should stop working the day you cancel, and the front desk should notice if a cancelled card is still being swiped.

You are three weeks into your role as access manager at Lakeshore Mutual Bank when the external auditor slides a spreadsheet across the table. Row 14 is Dana, a loan officer who moved to treasury two years ago. She can still approve loans, and now she can also start wire transfers. Row 19 is an account belonging to a teller who resigned in March. It logged in last Tuesday. The auditor waits, pen in hand, and asks the question you were hoping to avoid: "Walk me through how access is granted, changed and removed here, and who checks that it is still right?"

Identities are not static. People join, change jobs, take leave, become contractors and eventually leave, and systems and services come and go too. The identity and access provisioning lifecycle manages access through all of these stages. Many organizations describe it as joiner, mover, leaver (JML), and the exam expects you to know the typical risk at each step and the control that addresses it. The lifecycle applies to non-human identities as well: service accounts, application identities and the credentials that automated jobs use all need an owner, a purpose and an end date.
