Secure site and facility design

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed October 7, 2026 · Leer en español

Explain it simpler

Physical security is about keeping people, buildings and equipment safe from break-ins, theft, fire and floods. The best time to think about it is before you pick or build a building, because changing walls and doors later costs a lot. You start by choosing a safe location, away from flood zones and dangerous neighbors. Then you design the outside so it naturally discourages crime: good lighting, clear views and obvious signs that someone cares for the place. Inside, you add layers, like the layers of an onion, with the most valuable room in the middle. Above all, people must always be able to get out safely in an emergency. Think of a well-run school: one main entrance, visible play areas and clear exit routes.

The lease on a new office for Bluewater Credit Union goes to signature on Friday, and Anika from security has been invited to look at the floor plans on Wednesday, almost as an afterthought. She spots it immediately: the proposed server room sits on the ground floor against a glass wall facing the parking lot, beside the public lobby, with a sprinkler riser overhead. The facilities manager says it is the only space with enough power. Two days, a signature deadline and a design that will be very expensive to change later. What should Anika push for, and in what order of priority?

Physical security protects people, equipment and data from harms that no firewall can stop: intruders, theft, vandalism, fire, flood and accidents. The CISSP treats it as a design problem rather than an afterthought. The cheapest and most effective moment to secure a facility is before it is built, bought or leased, because moving a server room or adding a second entrance later is expensive. Above every other consideration sits one rule: human safety comes first. No physical control is acceptable if it traps people during an emergency. In risk terms, physical threats fall into a few families: natural (flood, earthquake, storm), human-caused intentional (theft, sabotage, workplace violence), human-caused accidental (spills, construction damage) and utility or supply failures. A good site and facility design addresses all of them, not just intruders.
